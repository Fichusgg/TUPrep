## Key ideas

- An argument is **valid** when the conclusion is true in *every* situation in which all premises are true. Validity is about form, not about whether the premises are actually true.
- To show **invalid**: find one counterexample row (premises all true, conclusion false).
- Valid patterns: modus ponens ($p\to q, p \vdash q$), modus tollens ($p\to q,\neg q\vdash\neg p$), hypothetical syllogism ($p\to q, q\to r\vdash p\to r$), disjunctive syllogism ($p\lor q,\neg p\vdash q$), constructive dilemma.
- Fallacies: **affirming the consequent** ($p\to q, q\vdash p$) and **denying the antecedent** ($p\to q,\neg p\vdash\neg q$).
- Proof patterns: direct proof, proof by contrapositive, proof by contradiction (assume the negation and derive ⊥), proof by cases, and counterexamples to refute "for all" claims.
- Categorical syllogisms ("All A are B; some C are A …"): draw Venn diagrams and actively try to build a counterexample.

## Common traps

- Judging validity by whether the conclusion sounds true in real life.
- Accepting "some A are B, some B are C, therefore some A are C" — the overlaps can be different individuals.
- Forgetting that a valid argument with a false premise can have a false conclusion.
- Proving the converse instead of the claim.
