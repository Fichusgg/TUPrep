## Key ideas

- Two formulas are **equivalent** ($\equiv$) when they have the same truth value in every row. Check by truth table or by rewriting with laws.
- Laws to know by heart: double negation, commutativity, associativity, **distribution** ($p \land (q \lor r) \equiv (p\land q)\lor(p\land r)$ and dually), **De Morgan** ($\neg(p\land q)\equiv \neg p\lor\neg q$, $\neg(p\lor q)\equiv\neg p\land\neg q$), **absorption** ($p \lor (p\land q)\equiv p$), identity/domination with ⊤ and ⊥.
- Implication rewrites: $p\to q \equiv \neg p \lor q \equiv \neg q \to \neg p$ (contrapositive). Also $\neg(p\to q)\equiv p\land\neg q$.
- $p \leftrightarrow q \equiv (p\to q)\land(q\to p)$, and $p \oplus q \equiv \neg(p\leftrightarrow q)$.
- The **converse** $q\to p$ and **inverse** $\neg p\to\neg q$ are equivalent to each other but *not* to $p\to q$.
- Simplify step by step, naming the law at each step. Fewer symbols at the end usually means fewer mistakes.

## Common traps

- Negating $p\to q$ as $\neg p\to\neg q$. The negation is $p\land\neg q$.
- Applying De Morgan without flipping the connective (¬ distributes *and* turns ∧ into ∨).
- Assuming that a law that works for ∧ automatically works for → (implication is not associative, not commutative).
- Treating "equivalent" as "one implies the other". Equivalence needs both directions.
