## Key ideas

- A **predicate** $P(x)$ becomes a proposition once $x$ is fixed. **Quantifiers** close it off: $\forall x\,P(x)$ ("for all") and $\exists x\,P(x)$ ("there is at least one").
- Negation pushes through and flips the quantifier: $\neg\forall x\,P(x) \equiv \exists x\,\neg P(x)$ and $\neg\exists x\,P(x)\equiv\forall x\,\neg P(x)$.
- "All A are B" is $\forall x\,(A(x)\to B(x))$ — with →. "Some A are B" is $\exists x\,(A(x)\land B(x))$ — with ∧.
- **Order matters** for mixed quantifiers: $\forall x\,\exists y\,L(x,y)$ (everyone loves someone) is weaker than $\exists y\,\forall x\,L(x,y)$ (someone is loved by everyone).
- Over a **small finite domain** you can check a statement by brute force: ∀ is a big ∧ over all elements, ∃ is a big ∨.
- A universal statement over an empty set is true (vacuous truth).

## Common traps

- Writing $\forall x\,(A(x)\land B(x))$ for "all A are B" — that says everything is both A and B.
- Writing $\exists x\,(A(x)\to B(x))$ for "some A are B" — this is true as soon as anything is not an A.
- Negating "every student passed some exam" as "no student passed any exam". The negation is "some student passed no exam".
- Swapping the order of ∀ and ∃ when translating.
